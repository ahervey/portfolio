Nº 01 · Avangrid Renewables · June – August 2026
MEW2

Adding an asset meant writing SQL by hand. I turned it into a guided form.

MEW2 sits on our SQL Server inventory of sites, devices and certificates. The forms were the easy part. The hard part was making that much data readable, and edits feel safe for people who'd never touched the database.

Role
Designer & developer (solo)
Timeline
June – August 2026
Collaborators
My manager, standing in for future users
Tools
React, Chakra UI, ASP.NET, Entity Framework, SQL Server
Contents

TL;DR

The pain

Creating an asset meant writing SQL by hand in SSMS, so only people comfortable with SQL could keep the inventory current.

What I did

Solo designer and developer: two-column browsing, validated forms and a review step before every commit.

The result

Launched to our team three months after kickoff; now rolling out to more teams.

The tension

Key question How might people who don't write SQL find the right asset, and change it with confidence?

Farms opens on 95 sites, each with servers, turbines and pads. Every decision below either makes that much data make sense, or makes sure nothing changes by accident.

  • Urgent no separate prototyping phase
  • No users yet my manager stood in
  • Generic data attribute records, raw columns
  • .NET server stays in the team's stack

Three decisions

With no time for a prototyping phase, I designed in code and walked each build through with my manager every week.

01

Two columns, then stop

Sites live in a persistent sidebar. Pick one and its devices open in a second column; the inspector shows the rest.

  1. Sites stay put, with a count.
  2. Devices open in a second column, with a back button instead of a third.
  3. Readable field names, mapped from raw attribute columns.
  4. Global search jumps straight to an asset.
Option I rejected

Finder-style columns that keep opening

The dashboards I studied on Dribbble and Contra lean on a hierarchy sidebar, and Miller columns can nest as deep as the data. I capped MEW2 at two so it never sprawls.

02

A form that knows the asset type

The blank query window became one form that swaps its fields by asset type and marks what's required.

  1. Pick a type: site, server, turbine, substation or certificate.
  2. Field and required counts up front.
  3. Validation names the exact missing field.
  4. Required fields filled, as you go.
What changed the design

A zip code that lost its zeros

A zip code set as a number input silently dropped leading zeros. Since then I treat field types and validation as part of the interface, not a back-end detail.

Tradeoff A mapping layer over raw columns is more code to maintain, but people see readable names and input is converted to the right type before saving.

03

Nothing changes silently

Edits collect as drafts, get reviewed side by side, then are committed together and logged in History.

  1. Pending count, Save and undo on every tab.
  2. Current against draft, for every edited field.
  3. Each change stays a draft until committed.

Tradeoff One API module handles every call's errors, retries and cancellation. More plumbing, but a slow connection never leaves a form half-broken.

Outcome

Keeping the inventory current no longer depends on someone who can write a query.

Creating an asset, before and afterWe didn't measure time saved, so here's the workflow itself.
Before: SQL in SSMSNow: MEW2
  • Write the SQL by hand, from a blank query window.→Pick an asset type and get a form with only that type's fields.
  • Know which raw columns each value belongs in.→Fill in readable field names, with required ones marked.
  • Run it, and the change is live.→Validation names what's missing, and edits wait for review before they're committed.

Reflections

  1. A manager is not a user

    With no existing users, my manager's weekly reviews were the whole feedback loop. They kept the build moving, but one person can't show me where a first-time user gets stuck.

    Next time I'd run usability tests with the teams now adopting MEW2, starting with creating an asset from scratch.

  2. Organizing beats decorating

    What I'm proudest of is how MEW2 surfaces information and explains what people can miss, like what a certificate is or whether a site is solar or wind. The visuals are plainer than I'd like.

    Next time I'd give the visual layer its own pass once the structure holds.

Nº 02 · Avangrid Renewables · August 2024 – February 2025
Alarms

The brief said “make it modern.” Its users needed to find an alarm, fast.

Hundreds of people use CORE Alarms to pull the alarm history of any site or front-end machine. I redesigned it around searching and reading results, built it solo in React and Node.js, and still maintain it.

Role
Sole designer & developer
Timeline
Built Aug 2024 – Feb 2025, maintained since
Collaborators
Godfrey (manager), Stephen (teammate) and the users we interviewed
Tools
Figma, React, Chakra UI, Node.js, Oracle, later SQL Server
Contents

TL;DR

The pain

It worked, but felt slow, couldn't sort or filter results, and never explained some columns.

What I did

Rebuilt it solo in React and Node.js with column filters, sorting, query validation and a searchable site picker.

The result

Shipped February 2025, replacing the old site. Since moved to SQL Server, still growing from user tickets.

The tension

The brief

“Make it look and feel more modern.” But the site already worked, so we asked the people using it what slowed them down.

What users told usWhere it went
  • It felt slow, especially on large results.→Pages, chunks and virtualized lists 01 02
  • It lacked proper sorting and filtering.→Filter by any value, pick columns, sort either way 01 02
  • Some things weren't explained, like certain columns.→Tooltips and validation that says what's wrong 03
How might engineers find the alarms they need in a long history, quickly and without guesswork?
  • Solo and out of practice with code
  • Keep what works every old query still runs
  • Two-month window per query

The same kind of query, old tool vs. new

Three decisions

I wireframed, rebuilt, then staged it for users and folded in their feedback.

01

Narrow the results, don't scan them

Filter any column by its values, sort either way, and keep active filters in view, so a long history shrinks to the rows you need.

  1. Active filters as chips you can clear.
  2. What's left: 43 results, 6 after filters.
  3. Sort either way.
  4. Pages instead of one endless table.

Tradeoff Results render a page at a time, in chunks, so the browser stays responsive on a busy two-month window.

02

One drawer for columns and filters

Sixteen columns is too many to read at once. One drawer holds both jobs: choose columns, then filter them.

  1. Choose which of the 16 columns to show.
  2. Filters read as inputs: “Any” until set, then green. Their open animation dropped from 200ms to about 80ms, since they're opened over and over.
  3. Reset or apply in one place.

Tradeoff Filter lists are virtualized, with fixed 40px options truncated and shown in full on hover. Long values are a little less visible, but big lists stay quick and never overlap.

03

Catch a bad query before it runs

The old form explained its syntax in a column of notes. The new one guides input instead.

  1. Quick ranges, since Chakra has no millisecond time picker.
  2. A type-to-filter site picker.
  3. Results stay put while you edit the query.
  4. Validation names what's missing; oversized queries are pointed to export.
Option I rejected

A long dropdown of sites

The alarm list has 100+ sites. A standard dropdown meant scrolling to find yours, so it became the keyboard-friendly picker in 2.

Outcome

  • Hundredsof people use it to look up alarm history
  • SQL Serverit outlived Oracle: I rewrote the queries, search and export
  • Steadier with big exports. A server-side row cap and CSV exports that stream instead of building in the browser.
  • More reliable. Validated settings and unit tests for filtering, export and validation.
Feedback that changed it

Tickets after launch

Users asked for alarm attributes the tool didn't show, so I added Reset Category and Category Code, and fixed reported bugs like station numbers not resolving to host names.

Reflections

  1. The brief is a starting point

    I was asked to make it look modern. The value was in sorting, filtering and explaining, and that only surfaced because we asked the people using it.

    Next time I'd time a few common lookups before the redesign, so I could show the difference instead of describing it.

  2. Solo, not alone

    My first project carried almost entirely by myself. My rusty programming came back faster than expected, and later generative AI (Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini) helped me plan refactors and chase bugs, which made the SQL Server move possible.

    Next time I'd get another designer's eyes on the UI early. I'm still finding things to polish.

Nº 03 · University of Tennessee · August – December 2022
MyChart

The patients who need MyChart most get lost in it

A ten-week class redesign. We traced the confusion to nested menus and rebuilt the app around bottom tabs and a dashboard about you. A Figma prototype, not yet user-tested.

Role
Lead designer: recruiting, hi-fi prototype
Timeline
10 weeks, Aug – Dec 2022
Team
University of Tennessee course team
Tools
Figma
Contents

The tension

North star Help someone like Mark find his lab results or his next appointment without getting lost.

MyChart already did the important things: messages, lab results, appointments. Layers of sub-menus buried them, for people who were mostly 40 or older, one relying on the app to manage a disability.

  • 10 weeks
  • 1 shared account
  • First Figma project
  • Existing blue brand
  • ~10people surveyed, most 40 or older
  • 10 wksfrom research to high-fidelity prototype
  • Untestedno usability test yet; test plan below

What we heard

We surveyed about ten people and ran contextual inquiries. Four findings set the agenda.

  1. Finding 01"Hard to navigate"

    Layers of sub-menus made it easy to get lost.

    Decision 01
  2. Finding 02The dashboard felt random

    Nothing on it was about them.

    Decision 02
  3. Finding 03The look didn't appeal

    But they came for their health, not color.

    Decision 03
  4. Finding 04Many found it confusing

    Most were 40 or older, so the app has to explain itself.

    Decision 04

"It does a lot of good, and I like that because I'm disabled and rely on this to stay on top of my health, but it is confusing at times and hard to use."

Survey respondent, 40–50 years old

Designing for Mark

We distilled the survey into one persona: Mark, 68, a working civil engineer who was hospitalized for heart palpitations, then missed follow-ups because of work.

Four decisions

  1. 01

    Bottom tabs over nested menus

    Fewer nested levels, with information where people look first, so Mark can manage on his own.

    Feedback that changed it

    "Tabs, not menus"

    People told us bottom tabs would beat nested menus. That moved navigation to the center of the redesign.

  2. 02

    A dashboard about you

    Upcoming appointments, recent lab work and visit results at a glance, so Mark isn't waiting in line to hear what he already knows.

  3. 03

    Calm white and blue

    Minimal and modern, built from simple community resources in Figma.

    Option we rejected

    A colorful makeover

    A brighter palette was the obvious fix for "didn't appeal." We stayed mostly white and blue: blue is tied to healthcare and already part of MyChart's brand.

  4. 04

    Show people around

    For the many who found the app confusing (finding 04), a first-run tutorial shows where everything lives. Hospital maps find the right room, and integrations connect accounts from other providers.

The prototype

Live prototype · Figma MyChart high-fidelity mock-up

Pan, zoom and click through the real design file.

Open in Figma

Reflections

  1. Consistency compounds

    My first UI/UX project and first time in Figma. Small inconsistencies between screens added up quickly.

    Next time I'd set up shared styles and components before drawing screens, and plan the semester backward from the deadline.

  2. A prototype isn't proof

    Every change traces back to something people told us, but we never tested the prototype with users, so I can't show it works better. I'd test it against the current app with people like Mark:

    • Find your most recent lab result.
    • Find when and where your next appointment is.
    • Find the right room using the hospital map.

    Next time I'd measure task success and time on both apps, and watch where people hesitate.

InTune

Listeners loved Wrapped, then waited a year for the next one

A three-designer class concept: listening stats you can check any week, and communities built around the artists you love. I'm now developing it into a real app.

Role
Lead designer
Timeline
10 weeks, Jan – May 2023
Team
Three designers: me, Holly Sarah Liban and Emma Thomas
Tools
Figma
Contents

The tension

North star Let serious listeners see their taste all year, and find the people who share it.

Wrapped and Replay were loved, but real insight arrived once a year. Last.fm had the depth and almost no social side. We had to learn whether people wanted something in between, and what would bring them back beyond the numbers.

  • 10 weeks
  • Unproven idea
  • Free Figma parts
  • 4listeners surveyed about how they track their music
  • 0 of 4had heard of Last.fm, the best-known stats app
  • NowI'm developing InTune into a real app

I led the high-fidelity prototype, the logo and design references. Holly Sarah Liban, Emma Thomas and I surveyed listeners and sketched the wireframes together.

What we found

We mapped what already existed, then asked four listeners how they track their music and find other fans.

  1. From 4 listenersThey wanted Wrapped all yearDecision 01
  2. From 4 listenersFew could find detailed stats when askedDecision 02
  3. From 4 listenersFinding fellow fans was hardDecision 03

"It's not something I ever really thought about, but I'd be interested to see my music tastes and how they change more than just at the end of the year."

Survey respondent, 18–20 years old

Four decisions

Numbers on our wireframes match the decisions below.

  1. 01

    Stats all year, not once a year

    Your history by day, week, year or all time, with this-week-versus-last comparisons and top genres, artists and albums.

  2. 02

    Connect once, tracked for you

    Pick your main service on the first screen and stats come to you. The picker goes beyond Spotify, where most fan-made tools stop.

  3. 03

    Communities around one artist

    Each community centers on one artist or genre, a place to talk releases with people who share your taste.

    Feedback that changed it

    The Twitter scroller

    One person found fellow fans by scrolling Twitter search and messaging them. So communities form around an artist, not general chat.

  4. 04

    A reason to come back

    Curated playlists, imagined as AI picks from your habits and similar listeners, plus MySpace-style profiles whose favorite track plays when someone visits.

    Option we rejected

    Stats and nothing else

    Last.fm already does deep analytics with almost no social side. Numbers alone give people little reason to come back daily.

The prototype

Live prototype · Figma InTune high-fidelity design

Pan, zoom and click through the real design file.

Open in Figma

Reflections

  1. Borrowed parts set the look

    Free community resources got us to high fidelity in ten weeks, but they decided more of the visual style than we did.

    Next time I'd design custom icons and vector graphics, so the visuals match the vision.

  2. Four voices is a start

    Four listeners were enough to show the gap between a yearly recap and Last.fm, not enough to prove people would switch apps for it. We never tested whether communities would actually bring people back.

    Next time I'd survey more widely, then test the community and analytics flows with listeners before polishing.

Autoethnography

A ten-week research and writing project on my own intersecting identities, and what it taught me about writing in the first person.

Please read before openingPanel 1 of 4

The assignment

I expected this class to be like others: pick a topic loosely related to me and write an essay about it. Instead, the direction was to write something deeply personal, in the first person. I'd always been taught to remove myself from my writing, so I was essentially reteaching myself how to write. It came with a lot of feelings that I was doing it wrong, but by the end of the course I was grateful for the chance to step that far out of my comfort zone.

au·to·eth·nog·ra·phy (n.) A form of ethnographic research in which the researcher connects personal experience to wider cultural, political and social meaning.

BackgroundPanel 2 of 4

Which story to tell

I hold many identities. I'm queer, Black, gender-nonconforming, Gen Z and more. When I was asked to write about an identity personal to me and its communities, I was stuck. Which one? Was any of it worth writing about? I chose my queer and Black identities, and how they intersected to create a sense of alienation from both, and a uniqueness I was both ashamed of and proud of.

It was heavy, and it didn't bring up the happiest emotions. I called on feelings of alienation from as early as kindergarten and traced how they rippled into who I am now and into my relationships. It wasn't pretty, but it was me, and that was what mattered most.

MethodPanel 3 of 4

Writing and researching

Autoethnography isn't always respected as research because it's an arts-based exploration of the self. I firmly believe these stories tell us about communities, and that first-hand accounts should count as legitimate sources.

I started by defining Blackness and queerness and how they relate. That was hard, because the experience of belonging to either varies depending on who you ask. Peer-reviewed material was less useful here than interviews and op-eds. I focused on how queerness isn't widely accepted in the Black community, how racism can hide inside the queer community, and how the othering both groups face doesn't always extend grace to people at that crossroads.

FindingsPanel 4 of 4

What I found

Once I started, writing it was surprisingly natural. I began like any other paper; the only difference was not taking myself out of it. Choosing which stories to tell was the real challenge, and I had a sense of those before I began.

My biggest takeaway was the beauty of combining art and research, and how writing truly changes you. For a while afterwards I wanted to be an English major. It also taught me to be articulate and to adapt my writing style to what a situation calls for, a skill I use every time I write up research or explain a design decision.
