Nº 01 · Avangrid Renewables · June – August 2026
MEW2

A guided interface for editing renewable asset data without writing SQL

MEW2 sits on top of our SQL Server databases so teams across Avangrid can maintain sites, devices and certificates through forms and a browsable tree instead of raw queries.

Role
Designer & developer (solo)
Timeline
June – August 2026
Collaborators
My manager
Tools
React, Chakra UI, ASP.NET, Entity Framework, SQL Server
Outcome
Launched to our team. It replaces direct SQL edits with a guided interface and is rolling out to more teams.
Contents

Overview

I was asked to build a website that works as an interface to our SQL Server, so people could change the database without touching SQL. The goal was to let several teams across the company maintain and update our renewable inventory and its data themselves.

I was both the designer and the developer. I built an ASP.NET back-end that uses Entity Framework to make changes without raw SQL, and a React front-end with four main areas:

  • Farms: our renewable sites and all of their devices.
  • Certificates: the certificates tied to each site.
  • History: what was created or modified, by whom and when.
  • Asset management: forms for creating new devices of each type directly in the database.
WebsiteC# / ASP.NET back-endReact front-end
Recipe card · Nº 01

MEW2, from scratch

Serves
Teams across Avangrid
Prep time
3 months

Ingredients

  • 1 cup competitive analysis (Dribbble & Contra)
  • 2 tbsp manager feedback, every iteration
  • 1 ASP.NET back-end
  • A splash of Entity Framework
  • React & Chakra UI, to taste
  • 2 Miller columns, no more

Method

  1. Study. Compare dashboards that hold lots of nested data.
  2. Sketch. A sidebar of sites that opens into their devices.
  3. Build. Edits that wait in a pending-changes panel until you commit.
  4. Review. Walk each build through with my manager, then iterate.

The problem

Key question What's the best way to display and surface this information?

Every designer wrestles with how an interface should look, but here the bigger question was how to surface a lot of information so it makes sense to a first-time user. That mattered most on the Farms page, which holds sites, turbines, pads and many other devices.

Research

Competitive analysis

MEW2 is an asset management dashboard, so I started by studying similar dashboards on Dribbble and Contra. I looked at how they organize large amounts of information and how people move between a high-level overview and specific details.

One pattern kept coming up: a persistent sidebar for hierarchical navigation. I adapted it so the sidebar lists each site, and clicking a site opens its child items. People can start at the top level and narrow down to exactly what they need without leaving the page or losing context.

Feedback loop

MEW2 was a brand-new tool, so there were no existing users to interview. Instead I reviewed each iteration with my manager, who represented the teams that would use it, and folded his feedback into the next build.

Key features

Review before you commit

Edits collect in a pending-changes panel. A global indicator shows unsaved work on every tab, and people can review each change, then commit or undo it.

Miller column navigation

Like macOS Finder: sites in the first column, their children in a second, capped at two columns with a back button so the layout never sprawls.

Validated asset creation

One form that changes its fields by asset type, marks required fields, and says clearly whether the asset was created or what still needs fixing.

History timeline

Cards that show what was created or changed, by whom, and when.

Tabs instead of pages

A button group swaps content in place instead of reloading the site, so moving between views feels instant.

Light and dark mode

A toggle so people can match the interface to their environment and preference.

Engineering for the interface

I chose React for the front-end because it was the framework I knew best, which let me move faster on the interface while keeping the server in the .NET stack the team already used.

On the server, one thin API controller hands work to a service layer split into small files by job: creation, attribute updates, batch saves, search and the asset catalog. Because the database stores many assets as generic attribute records, I wrote mapping layers that translate between the field names people see and the columns underneath, and convert text input into the right data types before saving.

On the front-end, logic lives in custom hooks such as useAssetCatalog, useAttributeEditor and useGlobalSearch. Every server call goes through one API module with shared error handling, retries with backoff and request cancellation, so a slow connection never leaves the UI in a broken state. A global search bar highlights matching text so people can jump straight to an asset.

Small decisions like an input type can quietly damage data. I now think about validation and field types as a UX problem, not just a back-end one.

Lesson learned: a zip code field set as a number input was silently dropping leading zeros.

Screens

Takeaways

The tool was needed urgently, so there was no separate prototyping phase. I designed as I built, shipped working versions quickly and iterated on my manager's feedback. I'm proud of how well it surfaces information and explains things people can miss, like what a certificate is or whether a site is solar or wind.

Nº 02 · Avangrid Renewables · August 2024 – February 2025
Alarms

Redesigning the historical alarms tool for our solar and wind sites

CORE Alarms lets engineers query Oracle for the alarm history of any site or front-end machine. I redesigned and rebuilt it on my own, from wireframe to a shipped React and Node.js app.

Role
Sole designer & developer
Timeline
August 2024 – February 2025
Collaborators
My manager, teammates and site users
Tools
Figma, React, Chakra UI, Node.js, Oracle
Outcome
Shipped. Replaced the old alarms site with faster filtering, sorting, input validation and a refreshed visual design.
Contents

Overview

My manager, Godfrey, looked for a project where I could put my UI/UX skills to work, and landed on the Oracle Alarms tool. It queries our Oracle database so people can view historical alarm data for a specific site or front-end machine (FE).

The rebuild was planned in Angular, but I moved it to React, which I knew best, and wrote the back-end in Node.js. As the sole designer and developer I:

  • Wireframed the direction for the redesign.
  • Built the site in React, following guidelines from Godfrey and Stephen on my team.
  • Found users to interview about the old site, the redesign and the features they wanted.
Node.js back-endReact front-endImproved visual experience
Recipe card · Nº 02

Alarms, refreshed

Serves
Engineers across the fleet
Prep time
Aug 2024 – Feb 2025

Ingredients

  • 1 cup contextual inquiry
  • ½ cup dashboard research
  • 1 Oracle database
  • 1 Node.js back-end
  • React & Chakra UI, generously
  • A pinch of TanStack virtualization
  • Avangrid brand colors, to taste

Method

  1. Listen. Ask people what slows them down in the old site.
  2. Wireframe. Sketch a softer, more open layout.
  3. Rebuild. Move it to React with filtering, sorting and validation.
  4. Test. Stage it for users, fold in feedback, ship.

The problem

Key question How can I make the UI feel more modern?

A more modern look was a specific requirement, and I agreed with it: the old site's visual design was one of my own pain points as a user. The site worked, so it didn't strictly need more than a UI refresh, but research showed there was more worth fixing.

Research

Looking at other dashboards

I studied dashboard designs to see which elements I wanted. What stood out were rounder type and buttons for a softer feel, brighter colors, and a less compact layout that feels more open.

What users told us

Contextual inquiry with people who use the tool surfaced three problems:

  • It felt slow, especially when a query returned a large dataset.
  • It lacked proper sorting and filtering.
  • Some things weren't explained, like what certain columns in the data meant.

Key features

Robust filtering

Filter results by any unique value in a column, choose which columns to show, and sort ascending or descending.

Error validation

Clear alerts when a query is incorrect, incomplete, or too large for the browser, with a prompt to export instead.

Small enhancements

Query all sites at once, a tooltip that explains the optional Tag field, and date selectors for quicker, error-free queries.

Design details

The interface uses one small visual vocabulary everywhere, defined in a Chakra theme, so new screens and the filter drawer read as part of the same page. I drew the colors from the Avangrid brand system.

#00A443 actions#00402A headings#E8F5EE hover#F1F3F5 page

Two layouts, remembered

Split puts results beside a 290px query panel; Stacked puts a full-width query bar above them. The choice is saved per browser, and the page still works if storage is blocked.

Searchable site picker

The alarm list has 100+ sites, so a long dropdown became a type-to-filter combobox with arrow-key and Enter support.

Filters that read as inputs

Outlined boxes show "Any" or "N selected" and fill green once set, so active filters are visible at a glance.

Results stay on screen

Editing the query doesn't clear the table. Only a new search replaces it.

Editable table view

Niche columns are hidden by default, and people can choose which columns matter to them.

Quick date ranges

Chakra has no millisecond time picker, so quick-range buttons sit under the date fields to reduce typing errors.

Fast menus

Menu transitions went from 200ms to about 80ms, because filter menus get opened over and over.

A fixed table viewport

The table scrolls inside a set area instead of stretching the page, and large datasets export to Excel.

Engineering

The front-end sends query parameters to the Node.js back-end, which assembles the Oracle query and returns results for rendering or export.

The hard part was table rendering. Queries can return a huge amount of data, so I paginated the table and processed results in chunks to keep the main thread responsive. Filtering also slowed down as data grew, so I virtualized the filter lists with TanStack. Every option is a fixed 40px tall, with long values truncated and shown in full on hover. That fixed a bug where wrapped options overlapped.

This was the first time I carried a project almost entirely on my own. I was out of practice with programming at first, but it came back faster than I expected. I'm grateful Godfrey found a project that matched my interests and gave me free rein with it.

MyChart

Simplifying a patient portal for the older users who rely on it most

A redesign of the MyChart app with streamlined menus, a more useful dashboard, hospital maps, integrations and onboarding, built as a high-fidelity Figma prototype.

Role
Lead designer
Timeline
10 weeks, Aug – Dec 2022
Team
University of Tennessee course team
Tools
Figma
Outcome
Wireframes, contextual inquiry results, and low- and high-fidelity prototypes. Open the Figma prototype ↗
Contents

Overview

Our class team was asked to redesign an app we already knew. A teammate suggested MyChart, a healthcare app for appointments, lab results and much more. We wanted to make it more accessible and modern, starting from the ground up.

As lead designer, I turned our vision and early prototypes into the high-fidelity design, and recruited the users we interviewed about the app and our ideas.

Recipe card · Nº 03

A calmer patient portal

Serves
Patients 40 and up
Prep time
10 weeks

Ingredients

  • 1 cup user interviews
  • 1 persona, Mark
  • 5 key features
  • White & blue, mostly white
  • A handful of Figma community resources

Method

  1. Map. List what the app already does.
  2. Interview. Find where people get lost.
  3. Distill. Turn what we heard into Mark.
  4. Prototype. Low- to high-fidelity in Figma.

The problem

Key question How can we streamline the app and make it less confusing?

Interviews pointed to one critical issue: navigation. Layers of sub-menus made it easy to get lost, which hit especially hard because so many of the app's users were older. Fixing navigation was also a chance to modernize the design.

Research

One of us had an account, so we mapped the app's existing features: direct messaging with providers, lab results as soon as they're available, and an appointment calendar showing when and where to go. Contextual inquiry then told us:

  • Most users were 40 or older.
  • Many found the app hard to navigate and confusing.
  • The visual design didn't appeal to them.
  • They felt bottom tabs would beat nested menus.
  • The dashboard felt random rather than personal.

"It does a lot of good, and I like that because I'm disabled and rely on this to stay on top of my health, but it is confusing at times and hard to use."

Survey respondent, 40–50 years old

Meet Mark

We organized everything from our inquiries into a persona of our typical user: Mark, a 68-year-old retired civil engineer managing a heart condition, who doesn't want to waste the time he has left waiting in line for health information he already knows.

Key features

A dashboard about you

Upcoming appointments, recent lab work and visit results, and anything else you'd want at a glance.

Hospital maps

A map of your provider's building, so finding the right room is as easy as finding the right address.

Simpler navigation

Fewer nested menus and information surfaced where people expect it.

Service integrations

Connect accounts from different healthcare providers in one place.

First-run tutorial

A welcome that shows what the app can do and where everything lives, before the next appointment.

Visual design

We went minimal and modern, mostly white and blue. Blue is closely tied to healthcare and was already part of MyChart's brand. We used simple, professional community resources in Figma and vector avatars for profile images.

InTune

A music social app for listeners who want more than a yearly Wrapped

InTune blends Last.fm-style listening analytics with Reddit-style communities, so serious listeners can track their taste all year and find people who share it.

Role
Lead designer
Timeline
10 weeks, Jan – May 2023
Team
University of Tennessee course team
Tools
Figma
Outcome
Wireframes, contextual inquiry results, a brand logo, and low- and high-fidelity prototypes. Open the Figma prototype ↗
Contents

Overview

We wanted an app that connects like-minded listeners and serious audiophiles. After settling on the concept, we prototyped in Figma and ran contextual inquiries to find out whether people needed it and would actually use it.

As lead designer, I built the high-fidelity prototype, researched potential users and design references for each page, designed the brand logo, and coordinated the wireframes with my team.

Recipe card · Nº 04

InTune, made to share

Serves
Serious music listeners
Prep time
10 weeks

Ingredients

  • 1 cup market research
  • ½ cup contextual inquiry
  • 1 survey
  • 6 hand-drawn wireframes
  • 1 brand logo
  • A dash of MySpace nostalgia

Method

  1. Research. See what Spotify, Apple Music and Last.fm already offer.
  2. Ask. Learn how listeners find stats and each other.
  3. Sketch. Wireframe analytics, communities and profiles.
  4. Polish. Build the high-fidelity prototype in Figma.

The problem

Key question What should the app look like, and what should it include?

Detailed analytics were a must for serious listeners. To keep people coming back beyond the numbers, we added curated playlists and communities around artists. The look would be minimal, with a splash of color to give each page its own identity.

Research

What already exists

  • Spotify offers Wrapped once a year but no detailed analytics on your library.
  • Apple Music's Replay updates weekly, but still lacks depth.
  • Last.fm has great analytics and almost no social features.
  • Fan-made tools like Receiptify are popular, and most support only Spotify, a sign of how accessible its API is.

What people told us

  • Most streamed often, and Spotify was the clear favorite.
  • Few knew how to find detailed analytics about their listening.
  • They loved Wrapped and Replay and wanted something like it year-round.
  • Nobody had heard of Last.fm, though they'd seen Receiptify posts.
  • Finding fans of the same artists was hard. One person scrolled Twitter search to find them.

"It's not something I ever really thought about, but I'd be interested to see my music tastes and how they change more than just at the end of the year."

Survey respondent, 18–20 years old

Key features

Detailed analytics

Continuous tracking by day, week, year or all time, with comparisons like this week versus last and your top genres, artists and albums.

Communities

Spaces around an artist or genre to chat with like-minded listeners about new releases.

Curated playlists

AI-generated recommendations based on your habits and similar listeners, to give people a reason to return.

Service integrations

Connect your favorite streaming service so your history is tracked accurately.

Profiles

An icon, a banner and a favorite track that plays when someone visits, in the spirit of MySpace.

Visual design

To move quickly, we built on community Figma resources, then shaped them into our own visual direction as wireframes became high-fidelity screens. Next time, I'd design custom icons and vector graphics so the visuals match the vision more closely.

Autoethnography

A ten-week research and writing project on my own intersecting identities, and what it taught me about writing in the first person.

Please read before openingPanel 1 of 4

The assignment

I expected this class to be like others: pick a topic loosely related to me and write an essay about it. Instead, the direction was to write something deeply personal, in the first person. I'd always been taught to remove myself from my writing, so I was essentially reteaching myself how to write. It came with a lot of feelings that I was doing it wrong, but by the end of the course I was grateful for the chance to step that far out of my comfort zone.

au·to·eth·nog·ra·phy (n.) A form of ethnographic research in which the researcher connects personal experience to wider cultural, political and social meaning.

BackgroundPanel 2 of 4

Which story to tell

I hold many identities. I'm queer, Black, gender-nonconforming, Gen Z and more. When I was asked to write about an identity personal to me and its communities, I was stuck. Which one? Was any of it worth writing about? I chose my queer and Black identities, and how they intersected to create a sense of alienation from both, and a uniqueness I was both ashamed of and proud of.

It was heavy, and it didn't bring up the happiest emotions. I called on feelings of alienation from as early as kindergarten and traced how they rippled into who I am now and into my relationships. It wasn't pretty, but it was me, and that was what mattered most.

MethodPanel 3 of 4

Writing and researching

Autoethnography isn't always respected as research because it's an arts-based exploration of the self. I firmly believe these stories tell us about communities, and that first-hand accounts should count as legitimate sources.

I started by defining Blackness and queerness and how they relate. That was hard, because the experience of belonging to either varies depending on who you ask. Peer-reviewed material was less useful here than interviews and op-eds. I focused on how queerness isn't widely accepted in the Black community, how racism can hide inside the queer community, and how the othering both groups face doesn't always extend grace to people at that crossroads.

FindingsPanel 4 of 4

What I found

Once I started, writing it was surprisingly natural. I began like any other paper; the only difference was not taking myself out of it. Choosing which stories to tell was the real challenge, and I had a sense of those before I began.

My biggest takeaway was the beauty of combining art and research, and how writing truly changes you. For a while afterwards I wanted to be an English major. It also taught me to be articulate and to adapt my writing style to what a situation calls for, a skill I use every time I write up research or explain a design decision.
